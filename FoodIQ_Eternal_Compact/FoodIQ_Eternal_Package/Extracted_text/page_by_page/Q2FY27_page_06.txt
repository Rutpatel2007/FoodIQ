Food delivery runs on deep integrations with restaurant kitchens, a large and highly efficient delivery
network that keeps delivery times short, and demand-supply balancing algorithms that get better with
scale. A delivered meal requires simultaneous coordination across the restaurant, kitchen, delivery
partner, routing, and payment, each step contingent on the last, all within minutes. Quick commerce is
similar - hyperscale inventory management and movement, real-time inventory checks against a specific
dark store, picker assignment, dispatch, all within a 10-minute delivery window. They are sequences of
tightly coupled, real-time handshakes, with low to no margin of error.

Agentic interfaces will need a single point of accountability for a transaction with multiple handshakes

When an order goes wrong, such as a missing item, a spilled meal or a damaged product, the customer
expects one party to fix it within minutes, and today that is us. That accountability is what customers trust
with a daily habit. An agent can place an order, but it cannot own the outcome; only whoever runs the
fulfilment can.

India has already tested what happens when you disaggregate these handshakes. ONDC separates
discovery, ordering, and fulfillment across independent providers. And it has struggled with food delivery,
because splitting real-time coordination across organisational boundaries introduces latency,
accountability gaps, and service failures at every junction. An AI agent orchestrating delivery
independently would face the same structural problem.

The further the transaction is from real-time, multi-party coordination, the easier it is to disintermediate.
Zomato and Blinkit sit at the other end of that spectrum.

The only way agents can disintermediate specialty products like Zomato and Blinkit is by handing over the
transaction completely to a service like ours, without making an attempt to disaggregate it. In case such a
scenario plays out, it will fast track consolidation of market share with the execution market leader.

Agents could end up making some categories “winner takes all”

Today, consumer habits and app-level inertia insulate every player in a competitive market. Agents could
erode that. An agent is a more demanding customer than a human: it optimises each transaction on actual
outcomes, not habit or brand loyalty, and routes to whichever platform is objectively better at that
moment. In categories with overlapping supply, this compresses the market toward the execution leader.

Agents will weaken content moats and strengthen supply chain moats

Some categories today are protected primarily by accumulated user content - reviews, ratings, curated
lists. Agents that synthesise information from across the web reduce the advantage of having built that
layer over many years. Categories that were historically hard to enter without a content moat become
more accessible to platforms with supply chain and fulfilment strengths.

The same shift applies to direct commerce - agents could help brands reach consumers without a
marketplace, but the brands would still have to fulfil the order itself, and that is expensive for most to do
alone. If direct commerce grows, the value shifts toward players with fulfillment infrastructure, regardless
of how the order originates.

This opens up possibilities for us to attack categories that have traditionally been unable to win due to the
incumbent’s existing content moats.
