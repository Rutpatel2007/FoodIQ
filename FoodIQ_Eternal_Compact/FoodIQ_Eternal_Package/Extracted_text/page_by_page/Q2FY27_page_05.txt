Q2. How are you thinking about the impact of AI agents on your businesses?
Deepinder: There is growing conversation around AI agents that shop on behalf of consumers. The
prevailing narrative is simplistic: agents will become the default commerce interface, and platforms that
fail to adapt will be disintermediated. This echoes the narrative from 10 years ago that super apps would
dominate everything.

We continue to believe vertical commerce, at least in some categories, will have an edge over super apps,
whether traditional or agentic. Blinkit and Zomato sit in the low-risk zone against the threat of agentic
commerce, and might even benefit from partial market share capture by agents in these categories.
District sits in the medium-to-high risk zone.

Let’s dig into various reasons that shape our thinking on this topic:​
​
Agents are great for tedious tasks. And most shopping is actually joyful for customers.

The strongest agentic use cases today are in categories where existing interfaces are genuinely painful:
too many clicks, too many tabs, too many providers to compare. Agents could make it easier for their users
in such cases – e.g. hotel bookings, restaurant reservations, flight bookings and check-ins.

But shopping, especially for food, is joyful and discovery-driven rather than tedious. Our recent consumer
surveys indicate that over 65% of Zomato app opens are exploratory. In such cases, the user does not
know what or where to order from. These users browse an average of 12 menus and over 100 food images
before ordering. That kind of serendipitous discovery is hard for an agent to enable. Users taking time to
order food is not a bug on Zomato, it is a feature. We have for years tried to make it faster for users to be
able to order from Zomato, to no avail. People want to browse and love to browse. We don’t see this
changing anytime soon. Conversational interfaces are not inherently superior to visual, browse-driven
ones for shopping, which is what we learnt from users who tried agentic ordering as early adopters.

Having said that, repeat orders or restocking is indeed a type of shopping that is tedious for customers. To
this extent, some of repeat orders or restocking behaviour might shift to agentic interfaces. We believe
that agentic-style interfaces built within Zomato and Blinkit apps will be able to cater to these customer
needs. Also, reordered carts are in low single digits on Zomato, as well as on Blinkit – so it’s barely a threat
to our overall business.

At worst, the narrow repeat-order use case leaks to agents, our in-app assistants serve even that. We
therefore expect all demand to stay within our apps as we continue to remain top of mind for our
customers.

Agents can only cause disruption in categories where they can fully disintermediate the marketplace

Even if agents become a meaningful commerce interface, the risk varies by category. The key distinction
is between platforms that intermediate pre-existing supply versus those whose infrastructure is a key
component of the supply value chain.

Where the underlying product exists independently of the platform, an agent can route around the
platform and go direct. These categories face real risk. District, our going-out business - restaurant
discovery, reservations, event ticketing - is partly one such category. A restaurant table or a non exclusive
concert ticket on District exists independently of our platform, and an agent can access it through other
channels.

Our food delivery and quick commerce businesses are protected, because they have embedded
infrastructure - they coordinate fulfilment in real time, and they own the outcome. Neither business is just
an intermediary. ​
